Offset vs Duration — which chart?

Same sample dataset in every panel: ~30 actions. Blue = healthy, amber = large offset (waited nearly as long as the action), red ▲ = offset > duration (impossible timing → data-integrity anomaly). The winning form makes those reds jump out with zero reading effort.

1 · Scatter, log–log, with an offset = duration reference line Recommended

Why this wins: the anomaly rule is a line (offset = duration). Anything above the diagonal is an overrun — you spot it instantly without reading a single number. Log axes spread values that span milliseconds→minutes (same reason the heatmap needed a log scale) so points don't pile up near the origin.

Benefits
  • Anomalies are geometric: above the line = broken. Instant.
  • Shows the whole relationship + outliers in one view.
  • Log–log handles the huge dynamic range gracefully.
  • Scales to hundreds of points.
Downsides
  • Overplotting if thousands of points (→ add opacity / hexbin).
  • Log axis needs a short "why log?" explanation for some users.
  • Two axes to read — slightly more than a bar chart.

2 · Scatter, linear axes Illustration

Shown to contrast #1. Same data, linear scale: the big-duration actions stretch the axes and everything small collapses into the bottom-left corner — exactly the "everything looks the same" problem you hit on the heatmap. Fine only if your durations are all within ~1 order of magnitude.

Benefits
  • No log-scale explanation needed.
  • Absolute distances read literally.
Downsides
  • Small/medium actions crush into a corner.
  • A few slow actions dominate the frame.

3 · Diverging bars: (offset − duration), sorted Strong alt

Best when the anomaly is the headline. One bar per action = offset minus duration. Bars crossing zero to the right (red) are overruns. Ranked, so the worst offenders sit together. Great for a "worst 20" list; weaker at showing the overall relationship.

Benefits
  • Overruns are unmissable (past the zero line).
  • Naturally ranked — triage worst-first.
  • One axis; trivial to read.
Downsides
  • Loses the offset-vs-duration relationship.
  • One row per action → doesn't scale past ~40.
  • Absolute Δ hides ratio (5s over on a 5s vs 5h action).

4 · Histogram of the ratio offset ÷ duration Distribution

Best for "is this dataset weird overall?" Bins every action by offset/duration. A healthy dataset clusters well under 1.0; the bars past the red 1.0 line are the impossible ones. Shows shape and tail at a glance, but you lose the identity of individual actions.

Benefits
  • Reveals the shape of the whole dataset fast.
  • Tail past 1.0 = the anomaly population.
  • Scales to any number of actions.
Downsides
  • Can't see which action a bar belongs to.
  • Bin width changes the story — needs care.

Also considered (not rendered)

Bubble chart — scatter #1 + a 3rd measure as dot size (e.g. widget count or session count). Adds a dimension for free, but size is hard to compare precisely and worsens overplotting. Good extension of #1, not a replacement.
Dumbbell / connected dots — per action, two dots (offset + duration) joined by a line. Elegant for a short, curated list (worst 15), but collapses at scale and, again, hides the relationship.
Hexbin / 2-D density — the answer if you have thousands of points and scatter #1 turns into a blob: bin the plane and shade by count. Same geometry (diagonal still means overrun) but aggregated.
Table + sparkline — the honest fallback when people need exact numbers to act on; pairs well beneath any chart.
